ESSENTIAL FEATURES
1. Interactive ward and neighbourhood explorer
- Select Trichy or Chennai, then a ward or neighbourhood.
- Click a map area to see its risk, recent observations, population exposure, and main contributing factors.
- Filter by risk level, date, and data availability.
- Show ward boundaries, nearby parks, water bodies, healthcare facilities, and cooling facilities when reliable data is available.
Why: The map must lead to useful information and actions, not simply display coloured areas.

2. Real heat conditions and data freshness
- Show current weather observations or forecasts separately from satellite-derived land surface temperature.
- Display when each dataset was last updated and where it came from.
- Show unavailable, stale, or incomplete data explicitly.
- Provide a timeline to compare available dates.
Why: Officials must know whether they're looking at current conditions, a forecast, or historical satellite observations. These are not interchangeable.



3. Ward risk profile with recommended action
Each ward should have a readable summary containing:
- Heat-risk category and score.
- Why the area is at risk.
- Who or what may be affected, based on available data.
- Suggested interventions and their urgency.
- Data confidence and the date of assessment.
Why: A government official should be able to understand a ward's situation without interpreting five statistical charts.



4. Action and intervention tracking
Make the Emergency Action Dispatcher operational:
- Create an action for a specific ward.
- Assign it to a department or responsible officer.
- Set priority, deadline, and status.
- Track acknowledgement, progress, completion, and evidence.
- Maintain an audit trail of who changed what.
Example: A high-risk ward needs additional drinking-water points. An officer assigns the task to the relevant team, which updates its progress and marks it completed.
Why: This is the biggest difference between a monitoring dashboard and a municipal decision-support application.

How each important feature should behave
A. Ward assessment: the most important screen
When an official selects a ward, display five sections:
1. Situation: Risk category, assessment date, land surface temperature estimate, and available weather context.
2. People and vulnerability: Population exposure, sensitive age groups, available socioeconomic indicators, and access to healthcare.
3. Why this ward? Leading heat and vulnerability factors, with explanations understandable to a non-specialist.
4. Recommended interventions: Relevant Heat Action Plan measures with reasons, limitations, and expected benefits where estimable.
5. Next step: A button to create a proposed intervention linked to the selected ward and its assessment.
Avoid showing a vulnerability score of 94/100, for example, without explaining its scale, calculation, data date, and interpretation. A high score should not automatically be presented as proof of an emergency.

B. Explainable AI: keep the science, change the presentation
Your SHAP implementation is valuable. The problem is not the graph itself; it is relying on the graph as the entire user experience.
The default explanation should answer:
- What factors are influencing the prediction?
- Which factors increase the predicted temperature, and which reduce it?
- How much does each factor contribute relative to the model's baseline?
- What practical intervention, if any, relates to those factors?
- How uncertain is the prediction?
Retain the SHAP waterfall plot and global feature-importance plots in an expandable Technical explanation section.
One scientific distinction matters here: SHAP explains how a model arrived at a prediction; it does not prove that changing a feature will produce the same temperature change in the real world. Intervention effects should be estimated separately and clearly labelled.

C. Real-Time Area Inspector: turn it into a scenario workspace
The existing sliders for NDVI, NDBI, building density, and distance to water can remain, but they should not be the primary controls.

D. Proposal builder: connect the analysis to an outcome
An official should be able to select Create proposal from a ward assessment and fill in:
- Selected city, ward, and assessment reference.
- Proposed intervention and justification.
- Priority and intended beneficiaries.
- Estimated cost and resource requirements, if available.
- Expected outcome and measurement method.
- Supporting map, evidence, and model explanation.
- Approval status and reviewer comments.
For your first working version, this can be a saved proposal with a simple approval simulation using local application records or a database. It does not require integrating with a real government dispatch system.
Use clear states such as Draft, Submitted, Approved, Rejected, and Completed. Record who changed the status and when. A proposal should not alter the original scientific assessment merely because it has been approved.

How to implement this without rebuilding everything
1. Foundation
Make existing data trustworthy and navigable
- Audit which datasets actually load in dashboard.py.
- Ensure city, ward, and grid selections stay synchronised.
- Add source dates, missing-data indicators, and a clear real-data/sample-data distinction.
- Link map selections to ward rankings and existing model outputs.
Completion test: selecting a ward changes every relevant panel to show that ward's data.

2. Usability
Build the ward assessment experience
- Add a detailed ward profile.
- Translate model outputs into plain-language explanations.
- Retain technical SHAP visualisations as optional details.
- Make rankings sortable and explain their underlying indicators.
Completion test: an official can explain why a ward is prioritised without interpreting raw ML charts.

3. Interaction
Connect scenarios to the existing models
- Implement baseline-versus-scenario comparisons.
- Validate the mapping between intervention targets and model features.
- Show actual prediction outputs, assumptions, and limitations.
- Save scenarios for comparison and reuse.
Completion test: a changed input produces a reproducible, model-backed result, not a hardcoded chart.

4.Workflow
Add persistent action proposals
- Create, save, edit, submit, and review interventions.
- Store records in a database.
- Add proposal statuses, reviewer comments, and a history of changes.
- Generate a report from the saved assessment and proposal.
Completion test: a proposal remains available after the dashboard restarts.

5.Validation
Prepare the complete demonstration
- Test both cities and representative wards.
- Test missing data, invalid inputs, and failed model predictions.
- Check that sample records cannot be mistaken for actual observations.
- Demonstrate the full assessment-to-proposal workflow.
Completion test: the system can demonstrate a complete scenario honestly, including its limitations.

Three scientific checks I would not skip
1. Comparable risk scores: Ensure exposure and vulnerability are on compatible, documented scales before applying your existing formula, \(\mathrm{HRI}=\sqrt{E\times V}\). Explain how the scores are normalised and what the final index means.
2. Population at risk: A vulnerability score or total population in a ward is not automatically a count of people at risk. Define the estimation method, avoid double counting across grid cells, and distinguish exposure from vulnerability.
3. Spatial validation and scenario validity: Retain your spatial block cross-validation. Do not interpret a high validation \(R^2\) as proof of real-time accuracy, and do not extrapolate the model beyond its training data without checking the input ranges and regional applicability.
