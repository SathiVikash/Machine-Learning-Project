# 2. Literature Survey

## 2.1 Urban Heat Island and Land Surface Temperature

Urban Heat Island (UHI) research has increasingly relied on remote sensing because satellite observations provide spatially continuous measurements of land surface conditions. Landsat is particularly useful because its Level-2 products provide surface temperature together with multispectral information that can be used to derive vegetation, built-up and water-related indices. The USGS states that Landsat surface-temperature products are specifically applicable to monitoring urban heat-island effects and are available globally. ([USGS][2])

Traditional urban heat studies generally examine relationships between LST and land-use/land-cover variables such as vegetation, built-up areas and water bodies. This provides a strong basis for understanding the physical drivers of urban heat but does not necessarily identify which neighbourhoods are most vulnerable to the consequences of that heat.

---

## 2.2 Machine Learning for Urban Heat Prediction

Machine learning has increasingly been used to model the nonlinear relationship between urban characteristics and LST.

For example, Mohammad et al. developed an ML-based framework for Ahmedabad, India, using multi-temporal Landsat data. Their study used an ANN-based Cellular Automata model for future LULC prediction and XGBoost regression to predict seasonal LST. The study demonstrated that machine learning could model future thermal conditions under projected land-use changes. ([ScienceDirect][3])

This establishes that **ML-based LST prediction in Indian cities is already an established research direction**. Therefore, simply implementing Random Forest or XGBoost to predict LST in another Indian city would provide limited novelty.

Similarly, recent work has applied machine learning to urban heat patterns in Delhi. A 2025 study used LSTM and Prophet to forecast temperature patterns using historical data from 2003–2021, demonstrating the use of temporal deep learning for urban heat forecasting. ([Springer Link][4])

---

## 2.3 Explainable Machine Learning for Urban Heat

The emergence of Explainable AI has provided a way to move beyond simply predicting temperature toward understanding **why** a model predicts higher heat.

Recent studies have used XGBoost combined with SHAP to identify the contribution of built-environment and landscape variables to urban thermal conditions. A 2025 study investigating the urban thermal environment demonstrated the use of XGBoost-SHAP to analyse spatial and temporal effects. ([MDPI][5])

A 2026 study examining Xi'an found nonlinear relationships between urban morphology and heat, with variables such as 3D form, NDVI and sky-view-related characteristics contributing to urban heat variation. ([ScienceDirect][6])

Similarly, research in Hue City combined Landsat-derived variables with Random Forest, XGBoost and deep neural networks and used SHAP to identify important heat-driving factors. Built-up density, road density and bare-soil-related variables were among the important predictors. ([CoLab][7])

These studies indicate that:

> **"XGBoost + SHAP + LST" alone is no longer a sufficient novelty claim.**

Your research therefore needs to move toward the **vulnerability and risk dimension**.

---

## 2.4 Urban Heat Vulnerability

Heat vulnerability differs from simple heat exposure because it considers characteristics of the population and its ability to cope with heat.

A recent 2025 study in Chongqing proposed an explainable ML framework that integrated heat exposure, accessibility to heat-mitigation resources and demographic characteristics. The researchers used XGBoost-SHAP to investigate demographic disparities in urban heat risk and found that vulnerable population groups experienced higher exposure and lower access to mitigation resources. ([ScienceDirect][8])

This is particularly relevant to your proposed project because it demonstrates that **heat risk should not be treated simply as a temperature map**.

However, the approach also means that your research cannot claim that "combining population data with LST" is completely new. Instead, your contribution should be more specific—for example:

* neighbourhood-scale vulnerability mapping for an Indian city;
* integration of environmental, built-form and population variables;
* comparison of interpretable and high-performing models;
* identification of nonlinear thresholds;
* analysis of spatial disparities;
* prioritization of intervention zones.

---

## 2.5 Indian Urban Heat Research

India provides a particularly relevant research setting because rapid urbanization has substantially altered land-use patterns and thermal environments.

Ahmedabad has already been studied using ML-based LULC and LST prediction. ([ScienceDirect][3])

Chennai has also been extensively studied using remote sensing. Recent research has examined long-term LST and UHI dynamics in Chennai from 2001–2022, identifying persistent high-temperature areas associated with built-up and paved surfaces. ([ScienceDirect][9])

More importantly, a 2026 study has already proposed a machine-learning-driven thermal-zonation framework for the Chennai Metropolitan Region using Landsat 9 and K-Means, classifying areas into low, moderate, high and extreme thermal zones. ([ScienceDirect][1])

Another recent study has investigated Chennai and Kolkata using ML, GIS and SHAP to identify cold-island cores and thermal corridors. ([ScienceDirect][10])

Therefore, if you choose Chennai, **you should not present the project as simply "creating a heat map of Chennai."** There is already recent work doing that.

Instead, your project should explicitly focus on:

> **heat exposure + population vulnerability + explainability + neighbourhood-level risk prioritization.**

# 13. How I suggest defining "Risk"

This is one of the most important methodological decisions.

Don't simply say:

> LST > 40°C = High Risk.

That's too simplistic.

Instead separate:

### Exposure

How hot is the location?

**LST / LST anomaly / heat hotspot intensity**

↓

### Vulnerability

Who lives there and how vulnerable are they?

Possible variables:

* Population density
* Elderly population
* Children
* socioeconomic indicators, if reliably available
* access to green spaces
* access to cooling/health facilities

↓

### Risk

Conceptually:

**Risk = Exposure × Vulnerability**

You can then classify the resulting spatial risk into:

> Low
> Moderate
> High
> Very High

This makes the project much more meaningful.
